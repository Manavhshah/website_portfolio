<Lede>
In January 2025 I spent a week in the Bay Area with UIUC's Silicon Valley Entrepreneurship Workshop, visiting startups and sitting with founders and operators who had built and lost real companies. I took notes obsessively. A year and a half later, these are the ones that held up.
</Lede>

## Speed is the only advantage

The CEO of an EV charging company told us the first idea had been an Airbnb for chargers. Five customers after weeks of effort. They pivoted to full charging software, learned it was a parking business rather than a gas-station business, and eventually ran thousands of stations at a reliability rate far above the industry's. His summary: "Your only advantage is your speed." And: "If you're not embarrassed by your first MVP, you are already too late."

I have since shipped things I was embarrassed by. It works.

## Investors want the business, engineers pitch the details

Every engineer in the room, me included, wanted to explain how the thing worked. The founders kept telling us the investor is buying the vision and the market. Fundraising gets exponentially harder as rounds progress, and then suddenly easier when a liquidity event is close. Strategic investors and institutional investors want completely different stories.

## T-shaped, then cone-shaped

A former Intel rotational-program engineer who later launched a phone in 2002 described his own arc: broad first, then deep. Know a little about everything, then go deep in a few places, then narrow into a cone. He also gave the most practical advice of the week: keep two running lists, one of ideas and one of emerging technologies, and go industry by industry asking what AI would disrupt.

## A company sells two things

A nuclear engineer who became COO of a company that raised four billion dollars and no longer exists put it plainly: a company sells a product and it sells shares of itself. When more effort goes into the second than the first, that is the problem. "Don't be distracted by the upfront money." He assigned us WeWork's S-1 as homework.

## Humility without being pushed around

An executive told us about being denied a raise early in her career for a reason that had nothing to do with her work. She asked for a new manager, said out loud that she would one day be that person's boss, and eventually was. The lesson she drew was not about revenge. It was about doing hard things with humility while refusing to shrink. Leadership is decisions during war; management is training before it.

Her framework for entrepreneurship was three overlapping circles: a market opportunity, a technology you love, and the ability to execute. The gap in the middle is the company.

## What I do differently now

- After every mentor conversation, I send notes and say what I learned. Then I follow up the next time with what I did about it.
- I ask whether I am having fun. A YC founder said it was the only question that predicted whether her cofounders would last.
- I read about the past when I want to understand the future. The founder of C3.ai said that is how you become a visionary. It sounded like a line. It has turned out to be true.

"Ideas mean nothing," the charging CEO said. What matters is what you do now.
